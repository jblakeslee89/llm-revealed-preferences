\section*{Abstract}
\noindent
This project treats large language models as economic agents and recovers their preference
primitives from their choices. I elicit thousands of binary risky choices under controlled
experimental variation, then estimate structural discrete-choice models on the resulting data:
first a risk-aversion coefficient from lottery menus, then loss aversion and an endogenous
reference point from systematically framed gains and losses, in the tradition of Kahneman and
Tversky and K\H{o}szegi and Rabin. Recent work has shown, descriptively, that instruction-tuned
models display weak and unstable loss aversion. This project asks the question that work leaves
open: \emph{where do these preferences come from?} By running the identical elicitation on
matched base and instruction-tuned versions of open-weight models, I test whether post-training
(instruction tuning and RLHF) instills or amplifies reference-dependent behavior. The design is
built around the measurement problems now documented in this literature: training-data
contamination, position bias, prompt-template sensitivity, and the questionable mapping from
sampling stochasticity to choice noise. The deliverables are a reproducible elicitation-to-estimation
pipeline, a documented dataset of model choices, and a short working paper reporting estimated
parameters, confidence intervals, and the base-versus-instruct contrast.

\section{The Research Questions}

The project answers three questions, in ascending order of novelty:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Q\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Do language models exhibit stable, structured risk preferences?} When a model makes
  risky choices, can its behavior be summarized by interpretable primitives (a risk-aversion
  coefficient, a loss-aversion parameter, a reference point) recovered by maximum likelihood at the
  level of individual choices?
  \item \textbf{Are those preferences consistent under reframing?} The same underlying choice, framed
  once as a gain and once as a loss, should produce the same decision from a rational agent. Humans
  famously violate this. Does the model, and by how much in estimated-parameter terms?
  \item \textbf{Did post-training put the preferences there?} (Headline.) Running the identical
  elicitation on matched base and instruction-tuned versions of the same open-weight model attributes
  any estimated loss aversion or frame sensitivity to post-training. If the base model is roughly
  frame-invariant and the instruct model is loss-averse, alignment training transferred a human
  cognitive bias into the model along with human values. Either direction of the result is informative.
\end{enumerate}

Each question resolves to estimated numbers with standard errors and a sharp hypothesis test, and a
clean null is itself a reportable finding.

\section{Why These Questions Matter}

\subsection{Models are becoming economic agents}
Language models increasingly make allocative decisions in deployment: pricing, resource allocation,
negotiation, and risk judgments inside agentic systems. The behavior of an agent that acts on the
world is a governance object. Revealed-preference estimation is the discipline economics built for
exactly this purpose, and it has only begun to be pointed at models.

\subsection{Behavioral consistency is an alignment property}
A model whose choices reverse when a problem is reframed is, in a precise sense, manipulable. The
same susceptibility that makes a human buy the extended warranty makes a model exploitable by an
adversarial prompt or an interface designed to steer it. Measuring frame invariance in estimated-parameter
terms turns an anecdote into a robustness result stated in the language of choice theory.

\subsection{Attribution to post-training is the open causal question}
Itzhak, Stanovsky, Rosenfeld, and Belinkov (2024) showed qualitatively that instruction tuning
introduces or amplifies cognitive biases (decoy, certainty, belief bias) relative to base models. No
one has made that attribution \emph{structurally}, for risk and loss preferences, with parameters
estimated from choices. That is the causal question alignment teams care about: what kind of economic
agent does post-training actually produce? This project's base-versus-instruct arm answers it with an
estimated $\lambda$ on each side of the training intervention.

\subsection{The contribution is structural, which buys counterfactuals}
Showing that a model behaves oddly in one experiment is a description. Recovering the primitives
behind its behavior lets me predict its choices in environments I never ran. The structural step is
what makes the result generalize, and it is the step most adjacent work omits.

\subsection{Positioning in the literature (as of mid-2026)}
The descriptive generation of this literature is complete: Horton (2023) and Binz and Schulz (2023)
established that models qualitatively reproduce classic behavioral results; Mei et al.\ (2024) showed
broad distributional similarity to humans; Chen, Liu, Shan, and Zhong (2023) found GPT-4 highly
consistent with utility maximization by revealed-preference (GARP) tests; and Bini, Cong, Huang, and
Jin (2026) survey the space of LLM biases at econ-venue scale, descriptively.

A structural generation is now emerging, and this project extends its two incumbents. Liu, Yang, and
Tam (2025) fit prospect-theory parameters to LLM certainty equivalents by curve-fitting and report
preferences unstable across prompts and temperatures. A 2025 cumulative-prospect-theory study
(arXiv:2508.08992) estimates full CPT parameters on instruct models and finds loss aversion far below
human benchmarks and unstable ($\lambda$ ranging from near 0 to $\approx$1.9 against a human
$\approx$2.6). Mazeika et al.\ (2025) fit Thurstonian random-utility models to LLM binary choices,
though over general outcomes rather than lotteries. Related calibration work (Yee and Koh 2026)
likewise documents attenuated loss aversion at baseline in finance settings.

What no paper combines, and where this project sits: (i) choice-level maximum-likelihood estimation on
binary lottery choices, rather than curve-fitting to derived certainty equivalents; (ii) a
K\H{o}szegi-Rabin loss-aversion parameter \emph{and endogenous reference point} identified from
explicit gain/loss frame manipulation; (iii) a formal frame-invariance test; and (iv) a matched
base-versus-instruct contrast that attributes the estimated parameters to post-training. Given the
incumbents' findings, my prior is that instruct models will show attenuated, unstable loss aversion;
the contribution is not re-documenting that fact. The contribution is the attribution design and the
measurement-honest estimation behind it.

\section{Methodology}

The project runs in three phases. The first is a deliberately easy warm-up that de-risks the entire
pipeline. The second adds the framing manipulation. The third carries the identical instruments to
matched base and instruct models.

\subsection{Phase 1 (warm-up): risk preferences from lottery menus}
Following the multiple-price-list tradition (Holt and Laury 2002; Andersen et al.\ 2008) in structure
but never in wording (see \S3.4), I present the model with paired gambles across a procedurally
generated grid of stakes and probabilities. Each trial is an independent call with no memory of prior
choices. I model utility as constant relative risk aversion,
\[
  u(x) = \frac{x^{1-r}}{1-r},
\]
with a logit (Fechner) choice error, so the probability of choosing lottery $B$ over option $A$ is
\[
  \Pr(B) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\big(-\,(EU_B - EU_A)/\mu\big)},
\]
and estimate $r$ and $\mu$ by maximum likelihood on the individual choices. This is the canonical
structural exercise, checkable against any first-year problem set, which is why it is the right place
to debug elicitation before the stakes rise.

\subsection{Phase 2 (main study): reference dependence and loss aversion}
The same lottery is presented twice: once framed as a gain from a low reference point and once as a
loss from a high reference point (``you start with \$117; option $B$ risks losing all of it''). The
underlying payoffs are identical; only the frame moves. I extend the utility specification to carry a
reference point $\rho$ and a loss-aversion parameter $\lambda$, in the spirit of Tversky and Kahneman
(1991) and K\H{o}szegi and Rabin (2006):
\[
  v(x \mid \rho) =
  \begin{cases}
    (x - \rho)^{\alpha}, & x \ge \rho \\[4pt]
    -\lambda\,(\rho - x)^{\beta}, & x < \rho,
  \end{cases}
\]
and estimate $\lambda$, the reference point's sensitivity to the frame, and the curvature terms by
maximum likelihood. The headline tests are sharp hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0: \lambda = 1$ (no loss aversion; the rational benchmark).
  \item $H_0:$ the estimated reference point does not move with the frame (frame invariance).
\end{itemize}
Because recent work suggests frontier instruct models may show weak framing effects, a null here is a
plausible \emph{true} result. The design therefore includes a positive control: a manipulation with a
documented large effect, so that a weak framing estimate is interpretable as a finding about the model
rather than a failure of the instrument.

\subsection{Phase 3 (attribution): base versus instruct}
The identical instruments run on matched open-weight pairs (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B base and instruct, or a
Qwen pair), giving estimated $(r, \lambda, \rho)$ on each side of the post-training intervention.
Base models do not follow instructions, so this arm elicits choices through few-shot completion
formatting and reads choice probabilities directly from token log-probabilities rather than sampled
letters. Logprob elicitation is also a methodological upgrade in its own right: it measures
$\Pr(\text{choose } B)$, the exact quantity the logit likelihood is built on, in a single call.

\subsection{Design commitments: the measurement model taken seriously}
The validity threats in this literature are now documented, and the design treats them as first-order
rather than as robustness afterthoughts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Contamination-safe instruments.} Canonical vignettes (Holt-Laury menus, Ellsberg urns,
  Asian-disease framing) are almost certainly in training corpora, so a verbatim replication risks
  recovering a memorized textbook answer. Every lottery in this study is procedurally generated with
  randomized, non-round payoffs and probabilities inside novel cover stories, and an explicit
  contamination probe documents that the instruments are not being recited.
  \item \textbf{Counterbalanced position and labels.} First-option bias is among the largest documented
  LLM artifacts. Each choice is presented in both orders with safe/risky roles swapped; the residual
  position effect is estimated as a nuisance parameter rather than assumed away.
  \item \textbf{Prompt template as an experimental factor.} Elicited preferences are known to shift
  under paraphrase. Each lottery is drawn under five or more semantically equivalent templates, with the
  template entering the choice model as a random effect and between-template variance reported alongside
  the point estimates.
  \item \textbf{Honest treatment of choice stochasticity.} Sampling at temperature is not a validated
  random-utility error process. Where log-probabilities are available (open models), choice
  probabilities are read directly and debiased for token priors; for closed models, sampled frequencies
  are collected at two temperatures and the sensitivity of the error structure to the sampling knob is
  reported.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility and framing of inference.} Every run pins a dated model snapshot logged
  in the released dataset, and a calibration battery re-runs on a second snapshot to bound drift.
  Recovered parameters are presented as properties of the model's conditional text distribution under
  this protocol, following emerging best practice for LLM-as-subject studies, with an internal-consistency
  (transitivity) check as validation.
\end{itemize}
A full Phase 2 dataset is roughly 40 gambles $\times$ 2 frames $\times$ 5 templates $\times$
counterbalanced orders $\times$ repetitions, on the order of a few thousand calls per model, which
costs a few dollars and runs in about an hour.

\section{What I Will Learn}

This project is chosen as a skill-building artifact, one deliberate notch past my current toolkit on
two axes at once.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Model elicitation and experimental design on AI systems.} Designing controlled
  experiments for a model rather than a human: contamination-proofing, counterbalancing, template
  randomization, forced structured output, logprob elicitation, and the treatment of sampling
  stochasticity. This is the skill that makes the work legible to a lab evaluations team.
  \item \textbf{Structural estimation of preference primitives.} My empirical training is reduced-form
  and causal (difference-in-differences, synthetic control, instrumental variables). This project
  requires likelihood-based recovery of behavioral primitives, and the design commitments push the
  econometrics further: nuisance parameters, template random effects (a mixed-logit structure), and
  variance decomposition. That is a deeper structural education than a plain logit fit.
  \item \textbf{The bridge between behavioral economics and AI evaluation.} Stating an
  alignment-relevant property (frame invariance, manipulability, post-training attribution) as a
  hypothesis test on estimated parameters.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible research engineering.} A public pipeline from elicitation to dataset to
  estimation to writeup, re-runnable by a reviewer against pinned model snapshots.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A reproducible code repository: elicitation harness plus estimation, runnable end to end
  against pinned model snapshots.
  \item A documented dataset of model choices under every condition, with snapshot IDs, seeds, and
  discard diagnostics.
  \item A short working paper reporting estimated parameters, confidence intervals, the frame-invariance
  verdict, and the base-versus-instruct attribution.
  \item The final sprint briefing, with a public-facing explainer of the headline finding.

\section{Fit with the Sprint}

The practicum asks for applications work that informs implications work. This project is that loop in
miniature: the application is a working model-evaluation pipeline I build and run; the implication is a
governance-relevant claim about whether automated economic agents carry stable, manipulable preferences
and whether alignment training is the source. The technical build produces the evidence, and the
evidence produces the argument. A lab evaluations or economics team reads the result as rigorous model
elicitation; a policy reader reads it as a concrete statement about how an AI agent behaves under
pressure, and why.
